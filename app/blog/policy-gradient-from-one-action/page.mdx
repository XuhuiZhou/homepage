# Policy Gradient From One Answer

<div className="not-prose mb-8 border-b border-zinc-200 pb-6">
  <div className="text-sm text-zinc-500">By Xuhui Zhou · Jul 16, 2026</div>
  <div className="mt-1 text-sm text-zinc-400 italic">
    One prompt, one answer, one reward, and the whole policy-gradient machine.
  </div>

Policy gradient looks scarier than it is because it usually arrives wearing too many hats:
trajectories, value functions, advantage estimates, PPO ratios, KL penalties, clipped objectives.
This post starts from the smallest possible LLM RL story and adds those hats one at a time.

The smallest story is this:

  <OneStepTrajectory />
</Figure>

A prompt $x$ arrives. The model samples a full answer $y$. A judge gives one final reward
$R(x,y)$. There is no intermediate reward, no environment state changing under our feet, and no
tool call. For now, the whole answer is one action.

The question is: **how do we change the model so answers with high reward become more likely?**

## The objective

For a fixed prompt $x$, the policy is the model distribution $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$.
Our goal is to make the expected reward large:

$$
J(\theta \mid x)
=
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[R(x,y)\right].
$$

If the answer space were tiny, we could write the expectation as a sum:

$$
J(\theta \mid x)
=
\sum_y \pi_\theta(y \mid x) R(x,y).
$$

This equation says something extremely plain: each possible answer has a probability and a score.
Expected reward is probability times score, added over possible answers.

Now we want the gradient:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta \mid x)
=
\nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y \mid x) R(x,y).
$$

The reward model or checker may be nondifferentiable. It may be a unit test, a human label, or an
LLM judge. So the gradient cannot usually flow _through_ $R(x,y)$. The only differentiable object we
control is the probability of sampling $y$.

That gives:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta \mid x)
=
\sum_y \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y \mid x) R(x,y).
$$

So far, nothing clever.

## The tiny magic trick

The trick is this identity:

$$
\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
=
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x).
$$

It is just the chain rule, because
$\nabla_\theta \log p = \frac{1}{p}\nabla_\theta p$.

Plug it in:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta \mid x)
&=
\sum_y
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
R(x,y) \\
&=
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[
R(x,y)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the basic policy-gradient estimator, often called REINFORCE.

Read the raw version like a child's rule for a model:

1. Sample an answer from yourself.
2. See how much reward it gets.
3. Larger reward gives a stronger push toward that sampled answer.
4. Soon we subtract a baseline, so below-expectation answers can be pushed down.

The gradient does not ask, "How do I differentiate the judge?" It asks, "Which sampled behavior
should become more or less likely?"

In the figure, the selected answer is the one we actually sampled. The update touches
$\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ for that sampled answer. The other answers matter only indirectly,
because increasing one probability forces the normalized distribution to move.

## The Monte Carlo version

In real LLM training, we do not sum over all possible answers. There are too many. We sample.

For a batch of prompts and sampled answers, the one-step estimator becomes:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
\approx
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{i=1}^{B}
R(x_i,y_i)
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x_i),
\quad
y_i \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i).
$$

This is Monte Carlo learning: try a few answers, score them, and use those scored samples as a noisy
estimate of the true expectation.

It is noisy because one answer is only one answer. If the model got lucky, the gradient may over-credit
it. If it got unlucky, the gradient may over-punish it. This is why baselines and advantages matter.

## The whole answer is secretly many token actions

Counting the full response as one action is a clean way to derive the math. For an autoregressive
language model, the full-response probability factors into token probabilities:

$$
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}).
$$

Taking logs turns the product into a sum:

$$
\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}).
$$

So the same one-step gradient can be written token-by-token:

$$
R(x,y)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
=
R(x,y)
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}).
$$

This is why a terminal reward in math or code RL gets copied across all generated tokens.
The model receives one final sticker, and every token in the sampled answer gets trained with that
same sticker.

This is valid, but it creates the classic credit-assignment problem. Maybe the final answer is wrong
because of one arithmetic token. The terminal reward does not know that. It pushes on the whole
sampled string.

## Baselines: the first sanity upgrade

Suppose a model gets reward $0.6$. Is that good? It depends. If most answers for this prompt get
$0.1$, then $0.6$ is great. If most answers get $0.9$, then $0.6$ is bad.

Policy gradient should care about **better than expected**, not just absolutely high.

So we subtract a baseline $b(x)$:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\bigl(R(x,y)-b(x)\bigr)
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
\right].
$$

Why is this allowed? Because if $b(x)$ does not depend on the sampled answer $y$, it adds zero in
expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
b(x)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)
\right]
&=
b(x)
\sum_y
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \\
&=
b(x)
\sum_y
\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \\
&=
b(x)
\nabla_\theta
\sum_y
\pi_\theta(y \mid x) \\
&=
b(x)\nabla_\theta 1 \\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The baseline does not change the expected gradient. It only makes the sample estimate less noisy.

The new multiplier is called the advantage:

$$
A(x,y)=R(x,y)-b(x).
$$

Plain meaning:

- $A(x,y) > 0$: this answer was better than expected, so push it up.
- $A(x,y) < 0$: this answer was worse than expected, so push it down.
- $A(x,y) \approx 0$: this answer was ordinary, so do not learn much from it.

## Advantage in the one-step world

In a one-step LLM trajectory, the action is the full answer $y$.
The action-value function is just the expected final reward after choosing that answer:

$$
Q(x,y)=R(x,y).
$$

The value function is the expected reward before seeing which answer we sampled:

$$
V(x)=
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}
\left[R(x,y)\right].
$$

So the advantage is:

$$
A(x,y)=Q(x,y)-V(x)=R(x,y)-V(x).
$$

This is the cleanest possible meaning of advantage: **how much better was this answer than the
model's usual answer for the same prompt?**

## From one answer to a real trajectory

Now expand the world back into normal RL. A trajectory is:

$$
\tau=(s_1,a_1,r_1,s_2,a_2,r_2,\ldots,s_T,a_T,r_T).
$$

The policy-gradient theorem gives the same skeleton:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)
A^\pi(s_t,a_t)
\right].
$$

All the earlier pieces are still there:

- $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ says what behavior to change.
- $A^\pi(s_t,a_t)$ says whether that behavior was better or worse than expected.
- The expectation says we learn by sampling trajectories from the policy.

For language models, the state $s_t$ is usually the prompt plus previous tokens, and the action
$a_t$ is the next token. If we treat the whole response as one action, $T=1$. If we treat each token
as an action, $T$ is the response length.

## Where PPO and GRPO enter

The equation above assumes samples come from the current policy $\pi_\theta$. In practice, we
usually sample a batch once from an old snapshot $\pi_{\text{old}}$, then perform several mini-updates
on that same batch. After the first update, the policy being optimized is no longer exactly the
policy that produced the data.

That is why PPO-style methods use an importance ratio:

$$
r_t(\theta)
=
\frac{
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)
}{
\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)
}.
$$

The unclipped surrogate is:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
r_t(\theta)A_t
\right].
$$

PPO clips the ratio so one batch cannot drag the policy too far:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\min
\left(
r_t(\theta)A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)A_t
\right)
\right].
$$

GRPO keeps the same policy-gradient skeleton but changes the baseline. Instead of learning a critic
$V(s)$, it samples a group of answers for the same prompt and compares each answer to the group:

$$
A_i
=
\frac{
R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)
}{
\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)
}.
$$

So the idea is still simple: make better-than-peer answers more likely, and worse-than-peer answers
less likely.

## The one sentence to keep

Policy gradient does not need to differentiate the reward. It differentiates the probability of the
sampled behavior.

The whole thing is:

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_\theta J
\approx
\text{advantage}
\times
\nabla_\theta \log \text{probability of the sampled action}
}
$$

In math notation:

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
\approx
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_i
A_i
\nabla_\theta
\log \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)
}
$$

In LLM words: sample an answer, score it, compare it to what was expected, and move the model toward
or away from the exact tokens it sampled.

Everything else - critic, group baseline, PPO ratio, clipping, KL regularization - is engineering
around this one sentence so the update is less noisy and less destructive.
